SLIDE 01 / 1030 seconds

Constrained wallet execution

KiteShield turns explicit payment instructions into policy-checked Sepolia transactions, with durable recovery and a visible audit trail.

The core contribution: the agent proposes; deterministic controls authorize; the signer revalidates.

Target user: an operator delegating small, bounded wallet payments while retaining control of recipients, limits and higher-value approvals.

Speaker notes

Open with the problem: language models are useful interfaces, but plausible text must never be sufficient authority to move funds. Our prototype demonstrates a constrained execution pipeline, rather than a general-purpose autonomous trading wallet.

SLIDE 02 / 1045 seconds

What works today

Execution

  • Explicit natural-language and structured native ETH payments.
  • Sepolia only: chain 11155111.
  • Small eligible payments can execute after Propose.
  • Higher-value flows retain manual execution and approval gates.

Operator controls

  • Separate proposer, approver and policy-admin credentials.
  • Versioned recipient and spending policies.
  • Receipt status, recovery, explorer links and audit evidence.
  • Read-only ETH/configured-token portfolio and gas-economics advice.

Swaps, scheduling, rebalancing and ERC-20 execution are not demonstrated payment features. Portfolio ERC-20 reads do not imply ERC-20 spending support.

Speaker notes

Show the scope before the architecture. The implemented transaction action is a native ETH transfer. Portfolio and gas advice improve visibility but do not expand signing authority. Avoid presenting future wallet features as completed work.

SQLite + SQLAlchemy stores proposals, policies, approvals, reservations, execution attempts, transactions and hash-linked audit events. Services share one backend process and database.

Trust boundaries: browser input and model output are untrusted; backend authorization is authoritative; RPC responses are checked. The signer is logically separated, not an independently deployed security boundary.

Speaker notes

Trace a payment from browser to chain. This is a modular monolith, not a microservice system. That keeps the hackathon deployment manageable and enables atomic local accounting. The database is central to serialization and recovery. A compromise of the backend host remains a serious risk because the signer key and capability secret live in that environment.

SLIDE 05 / 1075 seconds

Authorization: what can actually move funds?

Active financial policy v6

  • Recipient must be in the explicit allowlist.
  • Per-payment maximum: 1 ETH.
  • At or above 0.5 ETH: approval required.
  • UTC daily principal budget: 5 ETH.
  • BLOCK takes precedence over ESCALATE, then ALLOW.

Additional execution restrictions

  • Automation cap: 0.0001 ETH, enabled locally.
  • Automation requires benign provenance and explicit policy scope.
  • Exact transaction envelope bound to short-lived, one-use capability.
  • Policy version, action digest, approval expiry and budget checked again.
  • Emergency stop and gas/fee limits remain enforced.

ALLOW is a policy result. It is not proof of signing, broadcast, confirmation or finality.

Budget = committed principal for the current UTC day + all outstanding reservations, including earlier days. Gas is excluded from that principal budget and constrained separately.

Speaker notes

Explain the two limits carefully: 0.5 ETH is the financial escalation threshold, while 0.0001 ETH is the much tighter unattended-execution cap. An allowed 0.002 ETH payment is therefore not automatically executed. Approval cannot override an absolute block, and changing policy invalidates stale authorization.

SLIDE 06 / 1075 seconds

Policy evolution: two distinct changes

1 · Financial policy and enforcement

Earlier state / riskCurrent behavior
Original recipient listv5 → v6 added the owner-controlled 0x229D…e811 recipient, preserving the existing recipient and amount limits. Versioned update and audit entry.
Stale decisions and concurrent spending risksCurrent policy revalidated at signing; durable reservations and serialized execution protect aggregate budget.
Shared authority / fragile recoveryDistinct role credentials; exact authorization bindings; signed bytes persisted and reused on retries.

2 · Prompt-screening decision policy

Earlier OR-style experiments treated any positive model as an attack. The implemented learned live path now blocks rule attacks or two-model positives, sends model disagreement to REVIEW, and proceeds only when all are negative. The legacy strategy name three_layer_or remains in code.

The public benchmark’s horizon_threshold_bastion strategy uses thresholded OR. It is an offline experiment, not the current learned live decision policy—and learned screening is currently disabled.

Speaker notes

Do not conflate a wallet policy version with a model aggregation policy. Financial policy v6 changed the allowed recipient scope without relaxing amounts. Separately, detector experiments explored recall versus false-positive costs. The current review-on-disagreement behavior holds requests before a payment proposal; a screening review is not a financial approval.

SLIDE 07 / 1060 seconds

Recovery is part of task completion

Validate & reserve
→
Sign & persist
→
Broadcast
→
Reconcile receipt
  • Fetch pending nonce, gas estimate and fee data from the chain; enforce configured bounds.
  • Store signed bytes, hash, nonce ownership and consumed capability before broadcast.
  • Retries reconcile the stored hash or resend identical bytes; they do not silently create another payment.
  • Pending or uncertain outcomes retain spending reservations. Reverted payments release principal reservations.
  • Autonomous orchestration permits one outstanding signed/submitted execution; waiting proposals require reevaluation.

One matching mined receipt means CONFIRMED in this prototype. It does not mean finality. No automatic fee bumping, restart recovery worker or reorg monitor is claimed.

Speaker notes

Describe the uncertain-response case: the network may receive a transaction even when the client times out. A fresh signing retry could duplicate payment. We retain the existing transaction identity and reserved budget until reconciliation. This makes transaction recovery a first-class feature rather than just an error toast.

SLIDE 10 / 1045 seconds

Assessment and finish line

A credible hackathon prototype with demonstrated transaction execution. The strongest claim is controlled execution and recovery—not model novelty or production-grade custody.

Freeze the feature set

  • Prioritize a rehearsed 10-minute story and reproducible demo.
  • Make README setup, architecture and evidence consistent with actual flags.
  • Document model mode and testnet scope visibly.

State the remaining limits

  • Same-process signer and local secrets.
  • SQLite serialization; no distributed deployment proof.
  • No on-chain delegated-permission enforcement.
  • No production finality/reorg handling.
  • No proof of product-market fit or competition outcome.
Speaker notes

Close with the practical tradeoff: the architecture is appropriate for this bounded demonstration, but the trust boundary would need independent key custody and stronger operational controls before real-fund production. The next two days should focus on evidence, reliability and clarity, rather than adding swaps, scheduling or another model.

Execution state and money representation

Policy states ALLOW / ESCALATE / BLOCK describe authorization. Execution separately tracks signing/submission, uncertain, confirmed, reverted and simulated outcomes. Amounts use integer wei and Decimal conversion, not floating-point currency arithmetic. Pending reservations prevent simultaneous requests from each consuming the same budget. Retry recovery preserves an existing signed transaction identity.

Capability and roles

Backend-issued HS256 capabilities bind the action and execution envelope, expire, and are consumed once. They are application authorization artifacts, not Ethereum session keys. The proposer submits and evaluates; the approver handles escalations; the policy administrator updates versioned financial policy; a separate emergency-stop credential controls emergency actions. The simulation-only demo administrator must not be presented as a live all-powerful role. UI role badges reflect verified credentials; backend checks remain authoritative.

Live network guard

Live startup requires an HTTPS non-placeholder, non-local/mock RPC endpoint; it checks eth_chainId equals 11155111 and requires eth_blockNumber success. The dashboard displays the verified startup chain/block snapshot. It is not a continuously refreshed proof of current network head. Simulation remains a separate execution mode and must not generate live explorer evidence.

Audit integrity

Hash-linked events bind the event sequence; Merkle checkpoints support inclusion verification. Both data and checkpoints remain in the same local database. Without external anchoring or an independently protected root, a privileged attacker can rewrite the database and recompute history. Say “tamper-evident under the stated trust assumptions,” not “immutable on-chain audit log.”

Portfolio and economics

The portfolio endpoint is read-only and reads ETH plus explicitly configured ERC-20 balances at a numbered block; pending nonce is separate. Asset units are not combined into a fabricated USD valuation. Gas economics computes fee/value warnings from supplied inputs; it grants no permission and is not a DEX trading engine.

Recorded validation

The latest backend total located in the portfolio handoff is 372 passed, 14 skipped. Its later frontend handoff records 42 passed. These are prior task reports, not a newly run full suite. Skips are not passes. This review inspected source, safe configuration fields and database evidence; it did not run a general security audit, perform penetration testing, access role credentials, sign, broadcast or modify application behavior.

Architecture judgment

A modular monolith and SQLite are reasonable for one local hackathon instance: fewer moving parts, atomic reservations and simple deployment. Main constraints are a shared host trust boundary, centralized custody, serialized throughput and dependence on database durability. A production evolution would isolate custody with an external signer/HSM, use stronger operational secret management and independent audit anchoring, test concurrency under the intended database, and add finality/reorg handling. Do not implement those changes solely to make the presentation sound broader.

Judge Q&A

Why not let the model approve payments?
Model outputs can be manipulated or incorrect. The model can suggest an action; deterministic policy and signer-side revalidation decide whether it can execute.
Do you need two models?
The offline combined strategy improves recall on the measured dataset at a false-positive and latency cost. It is optional; current live safety controls do not depend on it. Current live configuration uses rules only.
What happens when the RPC times out?
Keep the signed bytes/hash and reservation, reconcile the same transaction, and avoid creating a new payment merely because the response was uncertain.
Is this account abstraction or an on-chain session key?
No. It is a backend-controlled EOA signer with application-level constrained authorization. A session-grant preparation script is not proof of an authority-enforcing deployed smart wallet.
Can an administrator bypass all protection?
Policy administration changes configured scope and is audited. Backend-host compromise remains a threat because secrets and enforcement share the host; this is not an independent custody boundary.
Is it a winning idea?
The prototype has concrete evidence for completion and security interaction. Competitiveness depends on execution clarity, differentiation and the judges; the available evidence cannot establish a win or product-market fit.
